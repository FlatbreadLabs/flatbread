## Where Proof is exposed

Worst first.

1. **An employment IP-assignment clause already covers Proof.** Many technology employment agreements assign to the employer any invention or software the employee creates that relates to the employer's business, or that is made with company time, equipment, or confidential information. GitHub's published Balanced Employee IP Agreement 2.0, which is a reusable template and is not Tony's contract, states the common pattern in public: the company owns IP created "within the scope of your employment or contract" regardless of time of day or whose computer was used; IP created outside that scope stays with the employee; IP created outside the job but related to the company's business is still the employee's, with a broad non-exclusive licence back to the company. IBM's current Business Conduct Guidelines, a real published employee policy, go further in one direction: an IBMer assigns to IBM "rights in intellectual property relating to IBM’s current or anticipated business activities you develop while an IBM employee, unless otherwise approved by management in accordance with our On Your Own Time – Guidelines for Developing IP." IBM also tells employees to get manager approval before outside activities that may compete, and to follow separate Open Source Participation Guidelines. Tony's employer is UNKNOWN. These documents are examples of what his contract may say, not what it does say.

   What would settle it: the signed employment agreement, any invention-assignment schedule, any "own time" or side-project carve-out, and a written answer from the employer's legal or open-source office on whether Proof is in or out of scope. An employment lawyer should read those clauses. This file is not that reading.

2. **A moonlighting or outside-income clause meets grant money.** GitHub Sponsors, FLOSS/fund, FUTO, NLnet, Sequoia, Manifund, Lightcone Commons, and EA Funds can all pay an individual. GitHub Sponsors makes Tony warrant that performing the terms "will not violate any other agreement or obligation between you and any third party." A clause that forbids outside paid work, or that requires prior written approval for it, would make that warranty false until approval exists. NLnet's sample MoU is a charitable donation, not employment, but the grantee still receives money and must report it to tax authorities. Prototype Fund will not pay a German employee unless the employer has released them, at least part-time.

   What would settle it: the outside-activity, conflict-of-interest, and secondary-employment clauses; any employee handbook rule on paid open source; and written approval that names the expected payers (GitHub Sponsors, a named grant, a fiscal host).

3. **A colleague commits from a work machine, on work time, or under a work email, and the employer owns that patch.** Microsoft's public contribution policy says small changes may go from a personal GitHub account, and larger changes need a business and legal review, including the project's CLA. Google's public patching page says Googlers must register a GitHub ID, should associate commits with a google.com email unless they had a prior history on that repo, may not sign an unlisted CLA without legal review, and should add a Google copyright notice on new files if the project permits it. IBM's Business Conduct Guidelines require IBMers who consume or contribute to open source to follow IBM's Open Source Participation Guidelines, and they assign related IP to IBM unless an On Your Own Time approval exists. A colleague who follows those rules may be contributing _as the employer_, not as a private person. The copyright in that patch may belong to Tony's employer or to the colleague's employer. The MIT licence can still cover the patch if the copyright owner grants it. The risk is a later claim that the owner never granted it, or that a CLA/DCO the colleague signed was false.

   What would settle it: each contributing colleague's own employment rules; whether they used a work account, a work laptop, or work hours; whether their employer has a standing CLA or contribution approval for Proof; and a DCO or equivalent on the commit that names the person who actually has the right to submit.

4. **Tony signs a funder's warranty he cannot truthfully make.** The live warranties already in reach are GitHub Terms of Service D.6 ("you have the right to license that Content under those terms"), GitHub Sponsors Additional Terms §4.1 (authority, no conflicting agreement, no third-party IP infringement), the Open Source Collective authority-and-indemnify clauses, the Sovereign Tech Fund "legally able to sign contracts" tick, Sequoia's "You do" ownership FAQ, and, if he ever signs one, an Apache-style Individual CLA §4. A false warranty is worse than a declined application. It can void payment and create a personal indemnity.

   What would settle it: the same documents as (1) and (2), plus a lawyer's view of whether he can sign each named sentence.

5. **A foundation home asks for an IP transfer he cannot give.** OpenJS, CNCF, and LF AI & Data all require, at some stage, a contribution agreement and a transfer of marks or project assets. OpenJS's IP policy says a DCO "will bind the individual contributor and, if applicable, their employer." CNCF inbound is Apache 2.0, not MIT. None of these is a cash grant. All of them assume an owner who can donate.

   What would settle it: written confirmation that Tony, not his employer, owns the name, domain, and copyright, or a deed of assignment from the employer.

6. **Double funding of the same activities.** Sovereign Tech Fund bars other _public_ money for the same proposed activities on the live page, and _public or private_ money on the November 2024 sample form. Prototype Fund bars double funding of the same idea. NLnet will not pay for work already finished. If Tony's employer already pays him to work on Proof, or later starts to, a funder who treats that as prior financing can claw back or refuse the grant.

   What would settle it: a clean split of work packages, written in the application, that the employer does not already pay for; and a re-read of the current Sovereign Tech portal form.

## What Tony must check

This is not legal advice. An employment lawyer should read the clauses before he signs a funder's IP warranty.

1. **The employment agreement.** Find the IP-assignment, invention-assignment, work-made-for-hire, and moral-rights clauses. Write down the exact trigger: "related to the company's business," "using company equipment," "during the term of employment," "on company time," or a broader "any invention." Note the governing law and any country-specific employee-invention statute the lawyer says may limit assignment.

2. **Any IP-assignment or invention-assignment schedule.** Some agreements attach a list of prior inventions. If Proof, Flatbread, or agent-memory work is not listed, ask the lawyer whether a late schedule is still useful or whether the assignment already attached.

3. **The employer's open-source contribution policy.** Ask whether one exists, who owns it (legal, OSPO, or engineering), and what it says about contributing to an _outside_ project the employee started before or during employment. Published examples of the kind of document to request: Microsoft's public contribution page (small-change exception vs larger change review), Google's patching page (CLA allowlist, google.com email, copyright-notice rule), and IBM's Business Conduct Guidelines plus the internal Open Source Participation Guidelines and On Your Own Time IP guidelines those Guidelines name. Tony's employer may have none of these. Ask anyway.

4. **Outside-activity, moonlighting, and conflict-of-interest rules.** Ask whether paid open-source work, unpaid open-source work, and receiving donations are treated differently. Ask whether GitHub Sponsors, a charitable grant, and a fiscal-host reimbursement are each "outside employment." Ask whether there is a pre-approval form and how long it takes.

5. **Equipment, time, and email.** Ask whether using a work laptop, a work GitHub SSO, a work email on commits, or any work hour taints the copyright. The safe default, until the policy says otherwise, is personal hardware, a personal GitHub account, a personal email in `Signed-off-by` lines, and no work hours.

2. **Adopt a Developer Certificate of Origin, not a CLA.** Effort: 3–5 hours. Add the DCO 1.1 text to `CONTRIBUTING.md`, as Node.js does (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nodejs/node/main/CONTRIBUTING.md). Tell contributors to use `git commit -s`.

   Why DCO fits a small MIT project: the Linux Foundation says the DCO is not a contract and not a licence. It is a one-way statement that the contributor has the right to submit the patch under the licence in the file. Contributors keep copyright. The project receives the same MIT rights it already gives outbound (inbound equals outbound). There is one DCO text, at https://developercertificate.org/, so a colleague's employer has already seen it. A CLA is a real contract, often with a broader copyright licence than MIT and a patent grant, and it needs a corporate signatory the day-to-day engineer usually is not. TypeScript is a Microsoft project. Proof is not. OpenJS and CNCF both say: use DCO unless there is a strong reason for a CLA. OpenJS prefers the DCO bot (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openjs-foundation/cross-project-council/main/governance/IP_POLICY_GUIDANCE.md). CNCF's 2021 foundation issue says the same and points at https://github.com/apps/dco.

Then use EasyCLA (https://easycla.lfx.linuxfoundation.org/) or CLA Assistant (https://github.com/cla-assistant/cla-assistant, hosted at https://cla-assistant.io).
