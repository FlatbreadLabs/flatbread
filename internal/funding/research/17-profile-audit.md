## Employer risks understated

1. **The issue starts before the first grant.** GitHub's Terms say that when someone adds content to a licensed repository, they license it on those terms and agree that they have the right to do so: https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service. If Tony's employer already owns some past Proof work, the risk is already in the public history. A new DCO does not cure an old ownership defect. Tony needs to identify which work was made when, on what equipment and account, and under which agreement. This is not legal advice; his agreement or a lawyer must answer it.

2. **GitHub Sponsors is not safe “after setup.”** Tony must warrant that he has authority, that performance does not violate any third-party agreement or obligation, and that sponsored content does not infringe third-party IP: https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-sponsors-additional-terms. Written employer clearance is not optional if his contract might assign inventions or restrict outside income. Country, tax, and payout support are also still unknown.

3. **A DCO is evidence from the contributor, not a grant of employer permission.** If a colleague contributes on work time, from a work account, or under an employer policy that assigns the patch, their sign-off may be false or incomplete. Before merging a non-trivial colleague patch, Proof needs an inbound-equals-outbound sentence, DCO checks, and a record that the contributor followed their own employer process. If an employer owns the patch, the employer may need to approve the MIT licence. Tony should not give legal advice to the contributor.

4. **A fiscal host moves risk; it does not erase it.** OSC assesses ownership and authority and expects the applicant to represent the project: https://docs.oscollective.org/interested-in-joining-osc/acceptance-criteria. Its sponsorship page tells applicants to read the full legal agreement: https://docs.oscollective.org/welcome-and-introduction-to-osc/terms-of-fiscal-sponsorship. The repository research found authority, no-conflict, and IP-indemnity terms in that agreement. If ownership is disputed, a host gives Tony another contract he may be unable to sign.

5. **Employer money can make Proof look controlled by the employer.** The Open Source Pledge excludes projects a paying company controls or trademarks and puts direct payments to employed maintainers outside its core scope: https://opensourcepledge.com/about/. Microsoft's fund requires company use but says the project cannot be owned by a Microsoft employee: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft-sponsorships/microsoft-foss-fund/main/README.md. Outreachy rejects communities tied too tightly to one company: https://www.outreachy.org/docs/community/. A company-branded roadmap, exclusive internal features, work-time-only contributors, or employer-owned marks could close routes that require independent work.

6. **Employer sponsorship may conflict with the clean “outside companies” story.** Sequoia says its fellowship supports open source outside company walls and says the fellow owns what they make: https://sequoiacap.com/oss. If Tony's employer claims Proof or directs its roadmap, Tony may not be able to make that ownership statement. 7. **Free credits can also be an employment question.** They can be personal benefits, require public recipient naming, or process project material under vendor data terms. Anthropic may identify Claude-for-OSS recipients publicly: https://www.anthropic.com/claude-for-oss-terms.
